# Backgrounder: Canada's National Security Strategy — History, Stakes and What to Watch

*As of October 29, 2026, before release. Every description of the new strategy's content is an expectation drawn from officials' previews, not a fact.*

**Bottom line:** No National Security Strategy text had been published or reported at time of writing, but every public signal says it is imminent. It would be Canada's first since *Securing an Open Society* (April 2004). Judge it mainly on whether it attaches money, legislation, deadlines, named owners and public reporting to its promises — where the 2004 policy and two decades of follow-on commitments fell short.

## 1. Status

- PCO held an embargoed technical briefing at 8 a.m. ET on Oct. 29, 2026, on "forthcoming Government of Canada measures related to Canada's national security." The advisory does not use the words "National Security Strategy."
- David Morrison told SECU the week of Oct. 19, in French, that he hoped the strategy would be public "within a week or two" (CP translation). PCO spokesperson Mélany Gauvin said it will be whole-of-society, focus on economic security and chart Canada's "strategic autonomy."
- February 2026 briefing notes for Public Safety Minister Gary Anandasangaree said it would, "for the first time, recognize the centrality of economic security to national security."
- A March 20, 2025 memo from then-Clerk John Hannaford, obtained by CP under the *Access to Information Act*, said changes in Canada's strategic environment made many draft assumptions "no longer valid." It was released heavily redacted.

## 3. The 2004 policy and what became of it

Three core interests: protecting Canada and Canadians; ensuring Canada is not a base for threats to allies; contributing to international security. Eight chapters; $690 million in new initiatives. Named threats included terrorism, proliferation, failed states, espionage, disasters, critical-infrastructure vulnerability, organized crime and pandemics. It named no hostile state.

| 2004 commitment | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Integrated Threat Assessment Centre | Created; now Integrated Terrorism Assessment Centre, remit narrowed |
| National Security Advisory Council | Created; shut Oct. 2012 |
| Cross-Cultural Roundtable on Security | Created; met infrequently |
| Arm's-length review of RCMP national security activities | Comprehensive review only with NSIRA (2019) |
| National Security Committee of Parliamentarians | Not implemented until 2017 |
| Public Health Agency of Canada | Created |
| Government Operations Centre; cyber strategy | Created / later delivered |

On openness, the policy promised oversight and review mechanisms and benchmarking against other countries. There was never a public implementation report or refresh.

## 4. The case for a new strategy

- **Hogue Commission, Recommendation 8:** a whole-of-government foreign interference strategy with a public timeline, integrated into a renewed NSS; plus a declassification system and public communications on protective measures.
- **uOttawa GSPIA Task Force (2022):** a thorough public review, a strategy fully funded from the outset, governance reform.
- **CIGI (Oct. 27, 2026; Carvin, Juneau, Neiman, Wark):** core principles of sovereignty enhancement, integrated decision-making and transparency; economic security tools, expanded foreign HUMINT, Arctic assessment, OSINT with guardrails, policing reform, space. Budget allocations required.
- **Wark (Oct. 28, 2026):** an opportunity for an independent Canadian vision, weighed against impacts on the Canada–U.S. intelligence relationship.

## 5. Carney-era run-up

- **Framing:** Davos "rupture"; Defence Industrial Strategy built on "strategic autonomy," which it says "does not mean isolation."
- **Machinery:** Morrison doubles as NSIA; Rochon handles domestic security. PCO has not said who owns the strategy, the NSC secretariat or intelligence priorities.
- **Foreign influence registry:** in force Aug. 4, 2026, Commissioner Anton Boegman; slow initial registrations.
- **Bill C-2/C-12/C-22:** border measures in force; lawful access split into a separate bill.
- **Bill C-40:** Defence Investment Agency as a Crown corporation; renamed and widened Defence Production Act; amends Schedule II of the *Access to Information Act*.
- **NATO intern case:** clearance granted despite a prior Public Service Commission fraud finding; screening "gaps."
- **Budget:** Nov. 5, 2026.

## 6. Allied comparisons

| Country | Transparency features |
|---|---|
| UK | NSS 2025; standing Joint Committee on the NSS; annual progress reporting since 2012 |
| New Zealand | NSS 2023; NZSIS annual unclassified threat report naming states |
| United States | Statutory NSS transmitted to Congress |
| Australia / Japan | Not researched in depth; verify before citing |

Canada has NSICOP and NSIRA, but neither is mandated to review an NSS, and there is no statutory NSS requirement.

## 7. Transparency benchmarks

1. A public definition of national security
2. A recurring unclassified threat assessment naming state actors
3. Published intelligence priorities (promised in the 2024 NSIA mandate letter)
4. Annual public implementation reports and a named parliamentary reviewer
5. Costing in the Nov. 5 budget
6. A fixed renewal cycle
7. A formal role for external advice
8. A declassification mechanism per Hogue

## 8. What to watch

- Is it the NSS? Title, length, classified annex?
- Which states are named; how is the United States characterized?
- Economic security tools
- Intelligence: foreign HUMINT service or expansion; OSINT framework
- Governance: owner, NSIA mandate, NSC charter
- Money and legislation
- Timelines, reporting cadence, parliamentary review
- Screening response to the NATO case
- Civil liberties and oversight language
- Space and Arctic
